A/B Test Design —
Does Our Alert Actually Work?

I built an automation that emails dealers when a customer bounces an EMI. It shipped to 100% of accounts from day one — so nobody, including me, could say whether it actually changes repayment behaviour. This is the experiment I designed to find out.

The Problem

Earlier in my automation work, I built a system that emails each dealership partner a list of their customers who just bounced an EMI, CC'ing the area and territory manager who owns that account. It runs on schedule and covers every eligible account — which is exactly the problem. It went to 100% of the population from day one, so there was never a control group, and no way to know what it actually buys us. It costs dealer and field-team attention every week, and nobody could say whether that attention was well spent.

"We were either spending that attention well, or spending it for nothing. Either way, we had no way to tell."

The Obvious Design Doesn't Work

The natural metric is cure rate — did the customer pay or not. I ran the power analysis before writing a line of the experiment, and it killed the naive design immediately. At a 45% baseline cure rate, detecting the +6pp lift that would justify the intervention's ongoing cost required 2,176 accounts — 23 months of accrual at this population's volume. A test that long is meaningless: portfolio mix and collections policy drift underneath it long before it finishes.

In a realistic 3-month window, the binary design could only detect a +16.6pp swing or larger. Anything smaller reads as "no effect" whether or not it's real — the test would just be measuring its own noise floor.

The Fix: Measure Money, Not a Yes/No

A cure flag throws away almost everything a partial payment tells you. Switching the primary metric to a continuous amount-recovered ratio — ₹ collected ÷ ₹ due within 30 days — uses the whole distribution instead of one bit per account. On the identical population, that redesign brought a conclusive test down to 260 accounts, roughly 2.8 months. Same cohort, same timeline constraint, a test that can actually reach a conclusion.

Finding the Contamination Problem

The treatment is dealer-level — one alert covers all of a dealer's bouncing customers — but I'd planned to randomise at the customer level. Checking the actual cohort surfaced a real problem: 12 of 24 dealers held accounts in both arms, meaning 81% of control accounts sat with a dealer who was actively being alerted about their other customers. A dealer told "three of your customers just bounced" may well chase all of theirs — which pushes the measured effect toward zero regardless of the true impact.

I considered randomising at the dealer level instead, which would remove the spillover cleanly — but with only 24 dealers, the effective sample collapses and the power problem I'd just solved comes right back. I kept the customer-level draw and pre-registered the result as an intent-to-treat lower bound, reporting the contamination rate alongside it, so a null result can never be misread as "the alert does nothing."

Design Discipline

1
Stratified rerandomisation

The first draw came back imbalanced on EMI amount (SMD 0.25). Because no outcome data existed yet and the acceptance criterion was fixed in advance, redrawing until covariates cleared |SMD| < 0.10 is legitimate — not p-hacking.

2
Pre-registered analysis plan

Primary: Welch t-test with covariate adjustment. Secondary: two-proportion z-test with Wilson intervals. Guardrail: escalation rate, capped at +2pp. Segments: Benjamini-Hochberg corrected, explicitly exploratory.

3
Fixed decision rule

The metric, sample size, and stopping point were all fixed before any data was seen — no stopping early on a good week, which is the most common way experiments produce results that don't replicate.

Current Status

The controlled test hasn't been run against real outcomes yet. I built and validated the full pipeline — cohort construction, anonymisation, power analysis, randomisation, and the pre-registered readout — against simulated outcomes so it's provably correct end to end before it touches production data. On that dry run, the result landed at p = 0.0513 with the confidence interval brushing zero: a useful reminder that the disciplined answer at a near-miss is "keep accruing," not "round up to significant."

The design is written up as a one-page decision memo, ready to take to the CRO for sign-off on withholding the alert from a randomised holdout for one quarter.
